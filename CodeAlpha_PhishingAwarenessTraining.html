01 — The Basics

What is phishing, really?

Phishing is a social-engineering attack where someone poses as a trusted contact — a bank, a coworker, a delivery service — to trick you into handing over credentials, money, or access. It doesn't break your systems. It talks its way past you.

01

Email phishing

Mass or targeted emails that mimic a real sender, pushing you toward a fake link or infected attachment.

02

Spear phishing

A personalized attack aimed at one person or company, using details gathered from LinkedIn, leaks, or social media.

03

Smishing & vishing

The same trick over SMS ("smishing") or a phone call ("vishing") — often faking a bank's fraud department.

02 — Recognize the Bait

Anatomy of a phishing email

Hover the dashed lines below — every one marks a classic red flag.

 Inbox
From: support@paypal-secure-verify.com
Subject: Your account will be suspended in 24 hours!

Dear Valued Customer,

We detected unusual activity on your account. Failure to verify your identity within 24 hours will result in permanent suspension.

Click here to verify your account →

Thank you,
The Security Team

1

Spoofed domain. "paypal-secure-verify.com" is not paypal.com. Always check the part right before the first single slash.

2

Manufactured urgency. Threats of account loss are designed to make you act before you think.

3

Generic greeting. Legitimate institutions you have an account with usually address you by name.

4

Mismatched links. The visible text says one thing; the actual URL underneath goes somewhere else.

5

No verifiable sender. No named person, job title, or way to independently confirm who sent it.

03 — Fake Websites

Before you type a password, check the address bar

A cloned login page can look pixel-perfect. The URL is the one thing an attacker can't fake without you noticing — if you know where to look.

Fake https://www.faceb00k-login.com/secure/signin
Fake http://192.168.4.20/bank-of-america/verify
Real https://www.facebook.com/login
✓

Check the padlock — then the domain

HTTPS just means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is trustworthy. Look at the actual domain name after the padlock, character by character.

✓

Watch for lookalike spelling

"paypa1.com", "amaz0n-support.net", or an extra hyphenated word are common tricks to fool a quick glance.

✓

Type it yourself

For banking or payment sites, type the address directly or use a saved bookmark instead of clicking a link from an email or message.

04 — Social Engineering

The psychology attackers rely on

Phishing rarely defeats technology — it defeats human instinct. These are the five levers attackers pull most often.

⏱

Urgency

"Act within 24 hours or your account is locked." Urgency shrinks the time you spend thinking.

🛡

Authority

Messages impersonating a CEO, the IT department, or a government agency exploit our instinct to comply with authority.

🎁

Reward & curiosity

"You've won a prize" or "Someone shared a document with you" — curiosity and greed override caution.

😨

Fear

Fake fraud alerts, legal threats, or virus warnings trigger a fight-or-flight response instead of a considered one.

🤝

Trust & familiarity

Impersonating a known coworker, vendor, or friend — often using details scraped from social media — to lower your guard.

05 — Real-World Cases

This isn't theoretical

Some of the largest breaches in history started with one convincing email.

2016

Google Docs share scam

A worm-like phishing email impersonated a "shared Google Doc" and spread through victims' entire contact lists within hours, harvesting account access at scale.

Spread to ~1M accounts in ~1 hour
2016

Business email compromise

Attackers impersonated a company executive by email, instructing finance staff to wire funds to a fraudulent account — a tactic still common today.

Losses regularly in the millions per incident
2020

Twitter VIP account breach

A vishing (voice-phishing) call to employees tricked staff into handing over internal tool access, letting attackers take over high-profile verified accounts.

130+ high-profile accounts compromised
06 — Defend Yourself

Best practices & tips

None of these require special tools — just habits.

✓

Pause before you click. Urgency is a warning sign, not a reason to hurry.

✓

Verify through a second channel. Call the person or company using a number you already trust — not one from the suspicious message.

✓

Hover before you click. Check where a link actually points before trusting the visible text.

✓

Enable multi-factor authentication. A stolen password alone shouldn't be enough to break in.

✓

Never share one-time codes. No legitimate bank or service will ever ask you to read a login code back to them.

✓

Report, don't just delete. Forward suspicious emails to your IT/security team so others get warned too.

✓

Keep software updated. Patches close the loopholes phishing payloads try to exploit.
